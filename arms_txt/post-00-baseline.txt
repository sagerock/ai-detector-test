The AI Detector Works. That's the Problem.

Law schools have run on an honor system for about as long as there have been law
schools. You sign something at the end saying the work is yours. Everyone knows a few
people are lying. Nobody can prove it. So the rule lands on everyone the same way,
which is another way of saying it lands on nobody.

That just changed, and I don't think most people have noticed yet.

A few weeks ago a law professor posted that AI detectors have gotten good. He'd spent
years telling people they were unreliable and he was changing his mind. He also
predicted that once professors catch on, a lot of them will go back and scan work
students already turned in. He meant it kindly. He was trying to warn students before
they got hurt.

The post bothered me for about a week before I could say why.

The rule isn't "no AI"

Here's what my legal writing class actually required. You write the memo in a vacuum.
Nobody else can look at it. Not your parents, not your brother or sister, not a
classmate, and definitely not an AI. You write it alone and you turn it in. There are
three of them and they're a big chunk of your grade.

So think about how many ways there are to break that rule.

Say your mother is a lawyer. Better, say she teaches legal writing. She reads your
memo the night before it's due and tells you your application section is all
conclusion and no analysis. You fix it. You turn it in.

Now say I ask an AI the same question, get the same answer, fix the same problem, and
turn it in.

Those are the same violation. One of them is invisible to every technology that
exists or ever could exist. The other one gets caught almost every time.

So I tested the detector

I'm a 2L, and I've spent twenty-something years in tech, so this is the rare thing I'm
actually positioned to check instead of just having opinions about.

I bought API access to Pangram, which is the detector everybody is pointing at right
now. Then I went and got the CourtListener bulk data, which is every published court
opinion in the country. It's 343 gigabytes.

I pulled 50 published opinions filed before January 2020. Real judges, real cases,
verifiable filing dates, all of it years before ChatGPT existed. I took a 400-word
chunk out of the middle of each one using the same rule every time, so I wasn't
picking flattering passages. Then I ran all 50 through the detector.

My theory going in was that legal writing would get flagged. Law school spends three
years teaching you to write in a structure. Rule, case illustration, application,
conclusion. Numbered elements. Parallel construction. It's formulaic on purpose. I
figured a machine trained to spot formulaic prose would light up on it.

I wrote that prediction down before I ran anything, which turned out to be lucky,
because I was completely wrong.

All 50 came back human

Not barely. The scale runs 0 to 1. The median score across 50 opinions was 0.0000.
The highest single score in the whole set was 0.0035. Every one of them came back
"Human Written" at high confidence. Opinions from the 1980s scored the same as
opinions from 2019.

So the thing I most wanted to be true isn't true. Legal writing doesn't read as AI.
The detector looked at fifty documents written by judges before any of this existed
and got fifty of them right.

I want to be really clear about this, because it's the opposite of what most people
writing about AI detection say. It works. I tried to break it with public
documents and it didn't break.

There's one caveat I'd put on their published error rates, and it's not a gotcha. To
claim a false positive rate you need a pile of text you know is human. For old
documents that's easy, because a filing date settles it. That's exactly why I used
pre-2020 court opinions. For anything written recently, somebody has to tell you they
wrote it themselves, and "I wrote this myself" turns out to be a slippery sentence.
Did you use spellcheck? Grammarly? Did you talk the problem through with something
before you started typing? Most people answer that question differently depending on
how it's asked, and not because they're lying. The categories are just mush. Every
accuracy number in this industry sits on top of that.

Which leaves the part that actually bothers me

My argument never needed the detector to be broken. It needs the detector to be
accurate and pointed at the wrong thing.

Because the tool doesn't detect help. It detects a machine. Those aren't the same
question, and the whole enforcement system is built on pretending they are.

My professor gave me back a memo with 21 comments on it. Things like "this is just
conclusion, not application" and "you need to use cases in your application section."
That's not proofreading. That's someone who knows the material telling me how to
think about the problem. It's also the entire point of the class, and I paid for it.

A parent who teaches legal writing gives exactly that feedback. An AI gives exactly
that feedback. My professor gives exactly that feedback. Three identical acts. One is
the curriculum, one is undetectable, and one ends your career before it starts.

And law school is graded on a curve. That part matters more than anything else here.
On a curve, you're not competing against a standard, you're competing against the
people sitting next to you. Every spot somebody gains, somebody else loses. Class
rank determines who interviews with the big firms. So when a rule is unenforceable
against one group and enforced perfectly against another, it isn't just unfair in the
abstract. It moves rank from one set of students to another set of students.

Which set? AI is the cheap version of a thing rich kids already had. If your family has
lawyers in it, you've always had someone to read your work. If it doesn't, twenty
dollars a month gets you a fraction of that. Detection destroys the cheap substitute
and leaves the expensive original completely untouched.

It's a technology that widens the gap it looks like it's closing.

The part that made me realize the line isn't where people think it is

I have ADHD, and I have accommodations. One of them is an AI tool that records my
lectures, transcribes them, and organizes them for me. My school approved it. It's in
my file.

So my school isn't against AI. My school has already given me one in writing.

The line isn't AI versus no AI. The line is AI that went through a process versus AI
that didn't. That's a paperwork distinction, not a teaching one. And getting through
that process takes a diagnosis, an evaluation, insurance, and enough executive
function to navigate a disability services office, which is a rough thing to require
from people whose disability is executive function.

Every legitimate way to get help is gated by something. Money, family, paperwork.
Detection only catches the ungated one.

Why schools do this anyway

I don't think the professors are villains here. I think they're stuck.

The fear is real and I want to say that plainly. Legal writing is a skill class. You
learn it by producing something bad, having it torn apart, and doing it again. If a
machine produces the thing, the learning doesn't happen, and you find that out in a
courtroom instead of a classroom. That's a legitimate worry.

The problem is what they did with it. Drawing a line is hard, so instead of drawing
one they banned the category. And a ban you can't enforce doesn't fail evenly. It
turns into a rule that binds the people who follow rules and the people without
resources, and floats right past everybody else. A clear permission would be better.
So would a restriction you could actually enforce. Absolute bans are the worst of the
three, because they produce the appearance of a standard and the reality of a lottery.

What would actually work

Ten minutes in a room with your professor. You defend your own memo out loud.

Why'd you frame the question that way. Why is this case distinguishable. What's the
weakest part of your analysis and how would the other side attack it.

If you can hold that up, you did the work, and it doesn't matter what touched the
keyboard. If you can't, you didn't, and it doesn't matter what a classifier says.

Notice what that catches that a detector never will. It catches your mother. A
student whose legal writing professor mom restructured their analysis folds under
three follow-up questions in exactly the same way a student who leaned on a chatbot
does. One test, every version of the violation, no software involved.

Law has done this before, incidentally. Oral disputation is the older assessment.
The written exam is the recent invention. And the highest-stakes moment in the whole
profession is still standing in front of an appellate panel that interrupts you
thirty seconds in, which is the one thing no tool can do for you.

So why doesn't anybody do it?

Because it costs faculty time, and a detector costs twenty dollars a month and
answers in nine seconds. That's the actual reason. Schools are buying a cheap answer
to a question they haven't defined.

The thing I keep circling back to

Writing was never really the point. It was the test. It was a good test because
faking it used to be expensive.

You can't tell whether you understand a case until you try to distinguish it on paper
and find out you can't. The prose is a diagnostic. That's what my professor was doing
when he wrote "this is just conclusion, not application." He wasn't grading my
sentences. He was telling me the thinking wasn't there yet, and the writing is how he
could see it.

So I don't think the answer is that writing stopped mattering. Briefs get read. Bad
writing loses cases. The answer is that we need a test that's still expensive to fake,
and speech under questioning is one.

But I'll be honest that I don't know where this lands. If AI can carry the prose, then
the bottleneck moves up to judgment, and maybe three years of teaching people to write
becomes three years of teaching people to think and the writing is a side effect. Or
maybe you can't separate those at all and I'm talking myself into something
convenient. I genuinely don't know. What I do know is that scanning documents doesn't
answer the question. It just makes it look like somebody did.
