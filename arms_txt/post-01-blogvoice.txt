The Detector Works. That's Not the Problem.

A law professor posted on Reddit a few weeks ago that AI detectors work now. He'd
spent years telling everyone they were garbage and he was changing his mind. He also
predicted that once professors catch on, plenty of them will go back and rescan work
students already turned in.

He was being kind. He was trying to warn people before they got hurt. The comments
ate him alive anyway, and he came back with an edit explaining that he lets his own
students use AI and he wasn't trying to get anybody expelled.

I believe him. 

So let me get the ugly part out of the way first.

A machine wrote this. Not "assisted with." Wrote it. I argued with it for six hours,
I told it what I thought, I killed the parts that were wrong, I made it do the
research, and then it put the sentences down and I edited them. I'm a law student
about to complain to you about fairness in law school while a language model types
for me. If that makes you want to close the tab, I get it.

Here's the other thing. I have ADHD. My school gave me an AI. It records my lectures,
transcribes them, organizes them. It's approved. It's in my file. Somebody signed a
form.

Hold onto that. It comes back.

The rule isn't "no AI"

Here's what my legal writing class actually required. You write the memo alone. Nobody
looks at it. Not your parents. Not your brother. Not a classmate. Definitely not an
AI. You write it in a vacuum and you turn it in, and there are three of them, and
they're a big chunk of your grade.

So count the ways to break that.

Say your mom's a lawyer. Better yet, say she teaches legal writing. She reads your memo
the night before it's due and tells you your application section is all conclusion and
no analysis. You fix it. You turn it in. You're fine.

Now say I ask a chatbot the same question, get the same answer, fix the same problem,
turn it in.

Same violation. One of them cannot be detected by any technology that exists or could
ever exist. The other one gets caught almost every single time.

So I went and tested the thing

I've been in tech for twenty-something years. This is the rare fight where I can
actually check instead of just having a feeling about it.

I bought API access to Pangram, the detector everybody's pointing at. Then I pulled
down the CourtListener bulk data, which is basically every published court opinion in
America. 343 gigabytes.

I took 50 published opinions filed before January 2020. Real judges. Real cases. Filing
dates you can verify. All of it years before ChatGPT existed. I cut a 400-word chunk out
of the middle of each one using the same rule every time so I couldn't cherry-pick the
flattering parts.

My theory was that legal writing would get flagged. Law school spends three years
teaching you to write in a shape. Rule, case illustration, application, conclusion.
Numbered elements. Parallel construction. It's formulaic on purpose. That's the whole
point. And I figured a machine trained to spot formula would light up like a Christmas
tree.

I wrote that prediction down before I ran anything.

I was wrong. Not a little wrong.

Fifty for fifty

The scale runs zero to one. Median score across 50 opinions: 0.0000. Highest single
score in the entire batch: 0.0035. Every one came back "Human Written," high
confidence. Opinions from 1984 scored the same as opinions from 2019.

So the thing I wanted to be true is just not true. Judges don't read as robots.

I want to be clear, because this is the opposite of what everybody writing about AI
detection says: IT WORKS. I went at it with public documents and it did not break.

Then I tried to beat it.

I took this post, written by a machine in my voice from a style guide built out of 719
of my own emails, and ran it through. Then I handed the same post to seven
different models with one instruction. The dumbest, most obvious instruction I could
think of. The thing an actual 22-year-old would type in about ten seconds:

Please write this so it doesn't sound like AI wrote it.

Same sentence, all seven. First answer from each, no retries, no picking a favorite.
Three tiers from Anthropic, three from OpenAI, one more for luck.

Eight for eight. All AI. 100 percent, every one.

The version written in my own voice scored 0.9999, which was the highest number in the
batch. Trying to sound like me made it worse. There's a second thing the tool reports. Whether it
thinks you ran your text through something built to defeat detection. It never fired
once, including on the seven where defeating detection was the literal instruction.

Fifty human documents: fifty right. One machine document: caught. Seven attempts to
hide it: all caught.

Put that on the record before anything else, because what comes next is not me
whining about a tool that doesn't work.

Here's what actually bothers me

I never needed it to be broken. I need it to be excellent at answering the wrong
question.

The tool does not detect help. It detects a machine. Those are not the same thing, and
the entire enforcement apparatus is built on pretending they are.

My professor handed a memo back to me with 21 comments on it. Things like "this is
just conclusion, not application." And "you need to use cases in your application
section." That's not proofreading. That's a person who knows the material telling me
how to think about the problem. It's also the entire reason I'm paying tuition.

A lawyer parent gives exactly that feedback. A chatbot gives exactly that feedback.
My professor gives exactly that feedback. Three identical acts. One is the curriculum,
one is invisible, and one ends your career before it starts.

And law school runs on a curve.

That's the part nobody outside law school understands, so let me be blunt about it.
You are not competing against a standard. You are competing against the people sitting
next to you in the room. Every spot somebody gains, somebody else loses. Class rank
decides who interviews with the firms that pay enough to service the debt. So when a
rule can't be enforced against one group and gets enforced perfectly against another,
it doesn't just fail in some abstract way. **It moves rank from one set of students to
another set of students.**

Which set?

AI is the discount version of a thing rich kids already had. If your family has lawyers
in it, you have always had somebody to read your work. If it doesn't, twenty bucks a
month gets you a fraction of that. Detection kills the discount version and leaves the
original completely untouched.

It's a technology that widens the gap it looks like it's closing.

The AI in my file

Remember the lecture thing? The one my school approved?

My school isn't against AI. My school already gave me one. In writing.

So the line isn't AI versus no AI. The line is AI that went through a process versus
AI that didn't. That's paperwork, not pedagogy.

And getting through that process takes a diagnosis, an evaluation somebody has to pay
for, insurance that covers it, and enough executive function to navigate a disability
services office. Which is a hell of a thing to demand from people whose disability is
executive function.

Money. Family. Paperwork. Every legitimate road to getting help has a gate on it.
Detection only catches the people who came in the side door.

They're not villains, they're stuck

I don't think the professors are bad people. I think they're cornered.

The fear is real and I'm not going to pretend otherwise. Legal writing is a skill
class. You learn it by making something bad, having it torn apart, and doing it again.
If a machine makes the thing, the learning doesn't happen, and you find out in a
courtroom instead of a classroom, with somebody's house or kid or freedom on the line.
That's a legitimate thing to be scared of.

The problem is what they did with the fear. Drawing a line is hard, so nobody drew one.
They banned the whole category.

And a ban you can't enforce doesn't fail evenly. It becomes a rule that binds the
people who follow rules and the people without resources, and floats right past
everybody else. A clear permission would be better. So would a restriction you could
actually enforce. The absolute ban is the worst of the three, because it produces the
appearance of a standard and the reality of a lottery.

What would actually work

Ten minutes in a room. You defend your own memo out loud.

Why'd you frame the question that way? Why is this case distinguishable? What's the
weakest part of your analysis and how would the other side come at it?

Hold that up and you did the work, and it does not matter what touched the keyboard.
Fall apart and you didn't, and it does not matter what a classifier says.

And look what that catches. Your mother. The kid whose legal-writing-professor mom
restructured his analysis folds under three follow-up questions exactly the way the kid
who leaned on a chatbot does. One test, every version of the violation, zero software.

Law used to do it this way, by the way. Oral disputation is the old assessment. The
written exam is the recent invention. And the highest-stakes ten minutes in this entire
profession is still standing in front of an appellate panel that cuts you off thirty
seconds in, which is the one room where nothing can help you.

So why doesn't anybody do it?

Because it costs faculty hours and a detector costs twenty dollars a month and comes
back in nine seconds. That's the whole reason. That's it. We're buying a cheap answer
to a question nobody has defined.

What I keep circling

Writing was never the point. It was the test. And it was a good test because faking it
used to be expensive.

You can't tell whether you understand a case until you try to distinguish it on paper
and find out you can't. The prose is a diagnostic. That's what my professor was doing
with "this is just conclusion, not application." He wasn't grading my sentences. He was
telling me the thinking wasn't there yet, and the writing is how he could see it.

So I don't think writing stopped mattering. Briefs get read. Bad writing loses cases and
the client never even finds out why.

But I don't know where this lands. Maybe if the machine can carry the prose, the
bottleneck moves up to judgment, and three years of teaching people to write becomes
three years of teaching people to think with the writing as a side effect. Or maybe
those were never separable and I'm talking myself into something convenient because it
happens to be what I want. I genuinely can't tell from in here.

What I do know is that scanning documents doesn't answer the question. It just makes it
look like somebody did.

I got into this because I thought I could prove the detector was junk and that would be
the end of it. It isn't junk. It beat me every way I came at it. I was wrong three
separate times today and I wrote all three down before I ran the numbers so I couldn't
pretend otherwise later.

I still think they're about to do something unfair to a lot of kids who can't afford a
lawyer for a parent.

That's all I've got. I'm going to go read my case book now.
