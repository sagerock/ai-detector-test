The AI Detector Actually Works. That's What Worries Me.

Law schools have run on an honor system for about as long as there have been law schools. You sign something at the end saying the work is yours. Everyone knows a few people are lying. Nobody can prove it. So the rule ends up applying to everyone equally, which in practice means it doesn't really apply to anyone.

That changed recently, and I don't think most people have clocked it yet.

A few weeks ago a law professor posted that AI detectors have gotten good — good enough that he was walking back years of telling students they were unreliable. He also guessed that once more professors figure this out, some of them will start scanning work that's already been turned in. He meant well. He was trying to warn people before they got burned.

I sat with that post for about a week before I figured out why it bothered me.

The rule was never "no AI"

Here's what my legal writing class actually required: you write the memo alone. No one else reads it before you submit. Not your parents, not a sibling, not a classmate, and definitely not an AI. Three of these memos, and together they're a huge chunk of the grade.

Now think about how many ways there are to break that rule without anyone ever knowing.

Say your mom's a lawyer. Say she used to teach legal writing, even. She reads your draft the night before it's due and tells you the application section is all conclusion, no actual analysis. You fix it. You turn it in.

Now say I ask a chatbot the same question, get more or less the same feedback, fix the same problem, and turn it in.

Same violation. Identical, really. One of them no technology on earth can catch. The other one gets flagged almost every single time.

So I actually tested it

I'm a 2L now, but I spent twenty-plus years in tech before that, so this is one of the few things about law school I'm actually equipped to check instead of just having a take on.

I paid for API access to Pangram — the detector everyone's talking about right now — and pulled the CourtListener bulk dataset, which is basically every published opinion in the country. 343 gigabytes of case law.

I grabbed 50 published opinions filed before January 2020. Real judges, real docket numbers, filing dates you can verify, all of it from before ChatGPT existed. From each one I pulled a 400-word chunk out of the middle, using the same rule every time so I wasn't cherry-picking clean passages. Then I ran all fifty through the detector.

Going in, my guess was that legal writing would trip it up. Three years of law school beats a structure into you — rule, illustration, application, conclusion, numbered elements, the same parallel sentence patterns over and over. It's formulaic by design. I figured a tool built to catch formulaic prose would flag at least some of it.

I wrote my prediction down before running anything. Good thing, too, because I was completely wrong.

Fifty for fifty, human

Not close, either. The scale runs from 0 to 1. Median score across all fifty opinions: 0.0000. The single highest score in the entire batch was 0.0035. Every one came back "Human Written," high confidence. Didn't matter if the opinion was from 2019 or 1985 — same result.

So the thing I was hoping to find isn't there. Legal writing doesn't read as machine-generated. I threw fifty documents at this thing that were written by judges before any of this technology existed, and it got all fifty right.

I want to say this plainly, because it cuts against almost everything else being written about AI detection: it works. I tried to break it with public documents, and I couldn't.

There's one asterisk worth putting on the accuracy numbers these companies publish, though it's not a gotcha. To measure a false positive rate, you need text you can be certain is human. Old opinions are easy — the filing date settles it, that's why I used them. But for anything recent, you're relying on someone telling you "I wrote this myself," and that phrase turns out to be a lot mushier than it sounds. Spellcheck? Grammarly? Talked it through with a chatbot before typing a word? People answer that differently depending on how you phrase the question, not because they're lying, just because the categories don't hold together. Every accuracy stat in this space sits on top of that mush.

Which is the actual problem

My argument was never that the detector is broken. It's that the detector is accurate and pointed at the wrong target.

It doesn't detect help. It detects a machine. Those are two different questions, and the entire enforcement system pretends they're the same one.

My professor handed back my memo with 21 comments. Things like "this is conclusion, not application" and "you need cases in here to support this." That's not line-editing. That's someone who knows the material walking me through how to think about the problem — which, incidentally, is the entire point of the class, and the reason I'm paying for it.

A parent who teaches legal writing gives you that exact feedback. A chatbot gives you that exact feedback. My professor gives you that exact feedback. Same act, three different sources. One's the curriculum. One's invisible. One ends your career before it starts.

And law school runs on a curve, which matters more than people give it credit for. On a curve you're not measured against a standard, you're measured against the person sitting next to you. Every rank you gain, somebody loses. Firms interview off class rank. So an unenforceable rule doesn't just fail quietly in the abstract — it actively moves rank from one group of students to another.

Guess which group. AI is just the discount version of something wealthier kids already had. Family full of lawyers? You've always had a built-in editor. Nobody in your family went to law school? Twenty bucks a month buys you a rough approximation. Detection kills the cheap substitute and leaves the expensive original sitting right there, completely untouched.

It's a tool that widens the exact gap it's supposedly closing.

Where I realized the line isn't where anyone thinks it is

I have ADHD, and I have accommodations for it. One of them is an AI tool that records lectures, transcribes them, and organizes the notes. My school signed off on this. It's on file.

So my school isn't anti-AI. It's already given me one, in writing.

The line was never "AI versus no AI." It's "AI that went through paperwork" versus "AI that didn't." That's a bureaucratic distinction, not a pedagogical one. And getting through that paperwork requires a diagnosis, an evaluation, insurance, and enough executive function to find and navigate a disability services office — which is a strange thing to demand from someone whose disability is, specifically, executive function.

Every legitimate path to getting help is gated by something — money, family connections, paperwork. Detection only ever catches the one path that isn't gated.

Why schools are doing this anyway

I don't think professors are the villains here. I think they're stuck.

The fear underneath all this is legitimate. Legal writing is a skill you build by writing something bad, getting it torn apart, and doing it again. If a machine does the writing, that loop never happens, and you find out the hard way — in a courtroom, not a classroom.

The problem isn't the concern. It's the response. Drawing a real line is hard, so instead schools banned the whole category. And a rule you can't actually enforce doesn't fail evenly — it just ends up binding the people who follow rules and the people with no other resources, while sliding right past everyone else. A clear, honest permission would be better than this. Even an enforceable restriction would be better. A flat ban is worse than both, because it gives you the appearance of a standard and the reality of a lottery.

What would actually work

Ten minutes in a room with your professor, defending your own memo out loud.

Why'd you frame the issue that way. Why's this case distinguishable. What's the weakest link in your argument, and how does opposing counsel go after it.

If you can answer that, you did the work — doesn't matter what touched the keyboard. If you can't, you didn't — doesn't matter what a classifier said.

Here's what that catches that no detector ever will: your mom. A student whose lawyer-mom rewrote their analysis folds under three follow-up questions the exact same way a student who copy-pasted from a chatbot does. One test. Every version of the violation. No software required.

This isn't even new, really. Oral argument is older than the written brief. And the single highest-stakes moment in the whole profession is still standing in front of an appellate panel that cuts you off thirty seconds in — the one thing no piece of software can do for you.

So why isn't anyone doing this instead?

Because it costs faculty hours, and a detector costs twenty bucks a month and spits out an answer in nine seconds. That's really the whole reason. Schools are buying a cheap answer to a question they never actually sat down and defined.

What I keep coming back to

The writing itself was never really the point. It was the test. And it was a good test, because faking it used to be expensive.

You don't find out whether you understand a case until you try to distinguish it on paper and discover you can't. The prose is diagnostic. That's what my professor was doing when he wrote "conclusion, not application" in the margin — he wasn't grading sentences, he was telling me the thinking underneath them wasn't there yet, and the writing was just how he could see that.

So I don't think writing stopped mattering. Briefs get read. Bad writing loses cases. What's changed is that we need a new test — one that's still expensive to fake — and being questioned out loud, on your feet, still is.

I'll admit I don't know exactly where this all lands. Maybe if AI can carry the prose, the real bottleneck moves up to judgment, and three years of learning to write quietly turns into three years of learning to think, with the writing just along for the ride. Or maybe those two things can't be pulled apart at all, and I'm just talking myself into a comfortable answer. I don't know. What I'm fairly sure of is that scanning documents for machine fingerprints doesn't answer the question. It just makes it look like somebody did.
