I Tried to Break an AI Detector. I Couldn't. That's Not the Good News.

Law schools have always run on an honor pledge. You sign a line at the bottom saying the work is yours, everybody assumes a handful of people are lying, and nobody can prove which ones. The rule technically applies to everyone, which in practice has meant it applied to nobody.

That's changing, and I don't think it's registered yet.

A few weeks back a law professor posted that AI detectors have gotten genuinely good. He'd spent a couple of years telling students they were junk science and he was publicly revising that. He also guessed that once word spreads, some professors are going to go back and run last semester's papers through one. He wasn't being menacing about it — the whole point of the post was to warn people before it happened to them.

It bugged me for about a week before I could articulate why.

The rule was never "no AI"

The actual language in my legal writing class: you write the memo alone. No one else reads it. Not your roommate, not a classmate, not your dad, and obviously not a chatbot. Three memos over the semester, and they're most of the grade.

Now count the ways to break that.

Say your mother is a lawyer. Say she teaches legal writing, even. She reads the memo the night before it's due and tells you the application section is all conclusion, no analysis — you're asserting the outcome instead of showing the work. You go fix it. You turn it in.

Or I ask Claude the same question, get the same note, fix the same problem, turn it in.

Same violation. Identical, as far as the rule is concerned. One of them cannot be detected by any technology that exists or plausibly ever will. The other one gets caught nearly every time.

So I went and tested it

I'm a 2L. Before this I spent a bit over twenty years in tech, so for once this is a thing I can actually check rather than just have a take about.

I bought API access to Pangram, which is the detector everyone's currently pointing at. Then I downloaded the CourtListener bulk data — every published opinion in the country, 343 GB of it.

I pulled fifty published opinions filed before January 2020. Real judges, real dockets, verifiable filing dates, all of it comfortably predating ChatGPT. I grabbed a 400-word chunk from the middle of each one using the same offset rule every time so I wasn't cherry-picking passages that flattered my hypothesis. Then I ran all fifty.

My prediction was that judicial writing would get flagged constantly. Law school spends three years drilling you into a format — rule, illustration, application, conclusion, numbered elements, parallel construction all the way down. It's formulaic on purpose. A classifier trained to notice formulaic prose seemed like it ought to choke on it.

I wrote the prediction down beforehand, which turned out to be lucky, because I was wrong in every direction.

Fifty for fifty

Not marginally. The scale is 0 to 1. Median score across the set: 0.0000. The single highest score anywhere in the fifty was 0.0035. Every one came back "Human Written," high confidence. A 1987 opinion scored the same as a 2019 one.

So the thing I wanted to be true isn't. Legal writing does not read as machine output, and the detector handled fifty documents written before any of this existed without a single miss.

I want to be blunt about that, because it cuts against basically everything else being written on this topic. It works. I tried to break it with public documents and it held.

The one asterisk I'd put on the published error rates isn't a gotcha, just a measurement problem. To claim a false positive rate you need a corpus you know is human. For old documents that's trivial — the filing date settles it, which is exactly why I used pre-2020 opinions. For anything written in the last two years you're relying on someone telling you they wrote it themselves, and it turns out "I wrote this myself" is a much softer sentence than it looks. Spellcheck? Grammarly? Did you talk the problem out with something before you opened the document? Ask people that three different ways and you'll get three different answers, and not because anyone's lying. The category is just genuinely blurry. Every accuracy figure in the industry is sitting on top of that blur.

The part that actually bothers me

My argument never depended on the detector being broken. It depends on the detector being accurate and aimed at the wrong target.

The tool doesn't detect help. It detects a machine. Those are different questions and the entire enforcement apparatus is built on treating them as one.

My professor handed back my first memo with 21 comments on it. "This is conclusion, not application." "You need to actually use the cases here." That isn't proofreading. That's a person who knows the material telling me how to think about the problem, and it's the whole reason I'm paying tuition.

A parent who teaches legal writing gives that exact feedback. An AI gives that exact feedback. My professor gives that exact feedback. One of those is the curriculum, one is invisible, and one is a hearing in front of the honor board.

And law school is curved, which is the part that turns this from an abstraction into a real transfer of something valuable. On a curve you aren't measured against a standard, you're measured against the twenty-five people in the room with you. Every seat someone climbs, somebody else drops. Rank determines who gets the callback interviews. So a rule that can't touch one group and lands perfectly on another doesn't just feel unfair — it moves class rank from one set of students to a different set.

And the direction it moves is not subtle. AI is a cheap knockoff of something wealthy students have always had. If there are lawyers in your family you've never lacked someone to read your work at 11pm. If there aren't, twenty bucks a month buys you a fraction of that. Detection eliminates the knockoff and leaves the original entirely alone.

Where the line actually is

I have ADHD and I have accommodations. One of them is an AI tool that records lectures, transcribes them, and organizes the transcripts for me. The school approved it. It's documented in my file.

So my school is not against AI. My school issued me one, in writing.

The line isn't AI versus no AI. It's AI that went through an approval process versus AI that didn't — which is an administrative distinction dressed up as a pedagogical one. Getting through that process requires a diagnosis, an evaluation, insurance that covers the evaluation, and enough executive function to navigate a disability services office, which is a slightly absurd thing to demand of people whose disability is executive function.

Every sanctioned route to getting help is gated by something. Money, family, paperwork, a psychologist's report. Detection only catches the ungated one.

Why schools are doing this

I don't think the professors are villains. I think they're cornered.

The underlying fear is legitimate and I want to say that clearly. Legal writing is a skills course. You learn it by producing something bad, watching it get taken apart, and doing it again. If a machine produces the artifact, the learning doesn't happen, and you discover that in front of a judge instead of in a classroom. That's a real thing to worry about.

The problem is the response. Drawing a defensible line is hard work, so instead of drawing one, schools banned the category. And an unenforceable ban doesn't fail neutrally — it binds the students who follow rules and the students without resources, and sails past everyone else. Explicit permission would be better. So would a narrower restriction you could actually police. A total ban is the worst of the three options, because it manufactures the appearance of a standard and delivers a lottery.

What I think would work

Ten minutes in an office. You defend your own memo out loud.

Why'd you frame the question that way? Why is Kowalski distinguishable? What's the weakest move in your analysis and how does opposing counsel go after it?

If you can hold up under that, you did the work, and it's irrelevant what touched the keyboard. If you can't, you didn't, and it's equally irrelevant what a classifier says.

And notice what that catches which no detector ever will. It catches your mother. The student whose legal-writing-professor mom reorganized their whole analysis collapses under three follow-ups in precisely the same way the student who leaned on a chatbot does. One assessment, every version of the violation, no software line item.

Law has done this before, for what it's worth. Oral disputation is the older format; the written exam is the recent innovation. And the highest-stakes twelve minutes in the profession is still standing at a lectern while three judges interrupt you before you finish your first sentence, which happens to be the one thing nothing can do on your behalf.

So why isn't anyone doing it? Faculty hours. A detector costs twenty dollars a month and returns a verdict in nine seconds. That's the whole explanation. Schools are buying a cheap answer to a question they haven't bothered to define.

What I keep coming back to

Writing was never the point. It was the test. It was a good test, for a long time, because faking it was expensive.

You can't find out whether you actually understand a case until you sit down to distinguish it and discover you can't. The prose is diagnostic. That's what my professor was doing with "this is conclusion, not application" — he wasn't grading my sentences, he was telling me the thinking wasn't there, and the writing was how he could see it.

So I don't think the conclusion is that writing stopped mattering. Briefs still get read. Bad ones still lose. The conclusion is that we need a test that's still expensive to fake, and talking under hostile questioning is one of the few left.

Though honestly I'm not sure where this ends up. If the machine can carry the prose, the bottleneck moves up to judgment, and maybe three years of teaching people to write turns into three years of teaching people to think with the writing as a byproduct. Or maybe those were never separable and I'm reasoning my way toward a conclusion that happens to be convenient for me. I don't know.

What I'm fairly confident about is that running documents through a scanner doesn't resolve any of it. It just makes it look like somebody did.
